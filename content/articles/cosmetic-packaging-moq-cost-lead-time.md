In most cosmetic projects, the packaging component is what sets the minimum order quantity, not the formula. A factory can batch a few hundred kilos of an emulsion with nothing more than a cleaning cycle between runs. A bottle cannot be made in a few hundred pieces: it is moulded, decorated and assembled in runs, and the setup cost — mould, screen, die, print plate — has to be paid for across the units you order.

This is why a quote for "30 ml serum" and a quote for "30 ml serum in an airless pump with hot-stamp logo" can differ by a factor of two, and why the second one usually has a launch date attached to a component delivery rather than to production capacity.

## The four tiers of component, and what each one costs you

Almost every packaging quote you receive is one of these four. Knowing which tier you are being offered explains the MOQ before the factory says a word.

**Stock.** An existing mould, held in inventory, available in a fixed range of colours. Lowest cost, shortest lead time, and the only realistic option for a first run under 5,000 units. The trade-off is differentiation: the same bottle is on a hundred other brands' shelves, and you may find it discontinued without notice.

**Semi-custom.** A stock mould with your choice of colour, finish or decoration. The shape is shared, the surface is yours. Setup charges appear per decoration method, and the MOQ roughly doubles.

**Custom mould.** A new tool built to your drawing. This is where brands get a genuinely ownable silhouette, and where the MOQ jumps to 10,000–30,000 units because the tool cost has to be recovered. Tooling itself is a six-to-ten week line item before a single unit is produced.

**Imported component.** A pump, dropper or cap from a specialist supplier. Often the best technical choice, and almost always the longest lead time. Import components act as a hard constraint on the whole schedule: if the pump arrives late, everything waits, and filling lines do not run on partial packaging.

## Decoration is where the minimums hide

A plain bottle has one MOQ. The same bottle with four decoration steps has four, and they are usually not the same number. Typical shop-floor realities:

- **Silk screen** needs a screen per colour, and each colour is a pass. Two colours means two setups and two rejection windows.
- **Hot stamping** needs a metal die, priced per design, and dies wear.
- **Pad printing** handles curved surfaces well but struggles with fine gradients and small type.
- **Spray coating and soft-touch** are batch processes — the whole lot is coated together, which sets a practical floor even if the component MOQ is lower.
- **Labels** avoid tooling entirely, and are the usual answer when volume is low. They also fail first in humid conditions, so test the adhesive against your actual storage.

If a quote gives you one MOQ for a decorated component, ask what it is quoted against. An MOQ quoted without a decoration specification is provisional, in exactly the same way an MOQ quoted without a component is.

## The compatibility test almost nobody runs early

Formula and component are usually developed in parallel by different people, then meet at the filling line. The failures that result are quiet and slow:

- **Stress cracking.** Some solvents, high-ethanol systems and concentrated surfactants attack moulded PET or polystyrene weeks after filling. The bottle looks perfect on the day and crazes in the warehouse.
- **Elastomer swelling.** Pump and dropper gaskets absorb oils and fragrance, swell, and start to weep or seize.
- **Adhesive failure.** Labels lift on condensation, and the residue is worse than the label.
- **Migration.** Fragrance and actives move into plastic, changing both the scent profile and the headspace.
- **Dead volume.** An airless pack that looks premium may retain several millilitres, which matters when the fill is 15 ml and the claim is 15 ml.

The only reliable answer is a filled sample in the actual component, stored at elevated temperature for at least four weeks. Ask for it before the bulk deposit, not after. A factory that resists this request is telling you something about how it handles everything else.

## Lead time, split properly

A single delivery date is a guess. Ask for the schedule in four parts, and hold the quote to it:

1. **Component lead time** — stock, semi-custom or moulded, plus decoration.
2. **Formula and compatibility** — development, revision, the filled stability sample.
3. **Bulk production** — batching, filling, sealing, labelling, QC release.
4. **Documentation and shipping** — certificates, export paperwork, transit.

For a typical semi-custom skincare launch, a realistic total from approved brief to goods on the water is twelve to eighteen weeks. Anything quoted materially under that is either using stock components throughout, or it is quoting the parts it controls and hoping the rest works out.

## A short checklist before you pay for packaging

- Component specification in writing, including resin, closure type, orifice size and fill volume tolerance.
- Decoration artwork approved at final size, not on a screen at 300 percent.
- Mould or tooling ownership stated, in your name, with transfer terms.
- Filled stability sample in the actual component, at least four weeks, with results.
- Over/under run policy: what happens to the extra units, and who pays for shortfalls.
- Drop test result for the packed shipper, not just the component.
- A named person accountable for the component, not the general sales inbox.
